Phishing emails get most of the attention, but they're just one delivery channel among many. The same scam shows up as a text message, a phone call, a fake job offer, a DM from a "friend," or increasingly, a deepfaked voice or video call. The channel keeps changing. What almost never changes is the underlying playbook: a small set of psychological levers that reliably get people to act before they think.

Learning to recognize those levers — rather than memorizing what last year's scam email looked like — is what protects you against the *next* scam, not just the last one.

## The five levers scammers rely on

1. **Urgency** — "Your account will be suspended in 24 hours," "final notice," "wire this today or the deal falls through." Urgency exists to short-circuit careful thinking. Legitimate organizations rarely require you to act in the next few minutes.

2. **Authority** — The message claims to be from your boss, your bank, the IRS, or your company's IT department. People are conditioned to comply with authority quickly, especially under pressure — which is exactly why scammers impersonate it.

3. **Familiarity / liking** — A message that sounds like a friend, a coworker, or someone likeable is far less likely to be scrutinized. Scammers research their targets (or just guess common relationships) specifically to earn this reduced scrutiny.

4. **Scarcity** — "Only 2 spots left," "one-time offer," "expires tonight." Scarcity manufactures a fear of missing out that pushes people to skip verification steps they'd normally take.

5. **Consensus / social proof** — "Everyone on the team already verified their info," "thousands of others have already claimed this." If it seems like everyone else already did the thing, it feels safer — and less necessary to check — even when no one actually did.

## Why this framing matters more than "spot the fake email"

Any single scam might combine two or three of these levers at once — for example, a fake "IT department" text (authority) demanding you reset your password *right now* (urgency) before your account is locked. The specific story will be new. The five levers underneath it almost never are.

## What to actually do

- **Notice the pressure, not just the story.** If a message is pushing you to act *fast* and *without checking*, treat that pressure itself as the warning sign — regardless of who it claims to be from or how it arrived.
- **Verify through a separate, known channel.** Don't reply to the message or call a number it gives you. Look up the organization's number yourself, or message the person through a channel you already know is theirs.
- **Slow down on purpose.** Legitimate requests can almost always wait a few minutes for you to verify. If something insists it can't, that's itself a signal.
